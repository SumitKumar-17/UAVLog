SEMANTIC LOG ANALYTICS FOR UAV FLIGHT LOGS
Speaker script for UAVLog_Presentation.tex (16 slides)
Presenter: Sumit Kumar (22CS30056)   Course: Advanced Database Management Systems
Total time: about 22 minutes (timings per slide below add up to 21:45), plus questions.

How to use this script: the SAY paragraphs are meant to be spoken as written. The ANALOGY lines are plain-English
explanations you can drop in when you see a blank face. Numbers are from the saved result files in bench/results/.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 1. TITLE                                                                          (0:45)
======================================================================================================
SAY: Good morning, everyone. My project is called Semantic Log Analytics for UAV flight logs. The one-line version
is this: I took drone flight logs and treated them as a database problem instead of a plotting problem. Everything
you will see runs on a single PostgreSQL instance, and every design choice is backed by a measurement.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 2. MOTIVATION AND PROBLEM STATEMENT                                               (1:45)
======================================================================================================
SAY: A drone writes a flight log every time it flies. In my data set, 300 real PX4 flights contain 70.7 million
telemetry samples and 3,401 events such as warnings and failure flags. That is three kinds of data at once. There is
a time series, there is free text, and there is a vendor dialect problem: PX4 and ArduPilot call the same quantity
by different names.

Today's tools show you one log at a time. But the questions people actually ask are about many flights, and they ask
them in words: "which flights lost a motor?" That mismatch is the problem I set out to solve.

I turned it into four research questions. One: how should high-rate telemetry be stored, and what does it cost? Two:
which vector index and which filtering strategy retrieve events best? Three: can a language model write safe and
correct SQL, and what does a semantic layer add? Four: does the pipeline survive failure and scale up to hundreds
of thousands of logs? Each question has an experiment later in the talk.

ANALOGY: Think of a flight log as a black box recorder for a drone. Opening one recorder is easy. Asking "which of
three hundred recorders ever heard a motor fail?" needs a library catalogue, not a recorder player.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 3. BACKGROUND: THE DATABASE BUILDING BLOCKS                                       (1:45)
======================================================================================================
SAY: Let me introduce the building blocks quickly. On the left is TimescaleDB, an extension that makes Postgres good
at time series. A hypertable looks like one table but is silently cut into time chunks. Old chunks can be moved into
the columnstore, which stores each column together and compresses it. A continuous aggregate is a rollup that the
database keeps up to date for you; I use one for one-second minimum, maximum and mean.

On the right is pgvector and pgvectorscale, which give Postgres similarity search. HNSW is a layered graph where you
hop towards the nearest neighbours. IVFFlat sorts vectors into clusters and only searches the closest few clusters.
DiskANN is a compressed graph designed to live on disk. And there is one known trap that becomes a headline result
later: when you add a filter, pgvector applies it after the index search, so a selective filter can return too few rows.

ANALOGY: A hypertable is a filing cabinet with one drawer per week. The columnstore is vacuum-packing the old drawers
so they take a thirtieth of the space. HNSW is asking a friend of a friend of a friend for directions: each hop gets
you closer, and you never walk the whole city. The filter trap is asking for directions to "a vegetarian restaurant"
only after the friends have already pointed you to five steakhouses.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 4. THE GAP AND THE ARCHITECTURE                                                   (1:15)
======================================================================================================
SAY: Here is the gap. Log viewers show one flight and know nothing about meaning. Time-series databases store the
numbers but have no ontology or vendor mapping. Natural-language-to-SQL demos exist, but they are rarely guarded or
measured. To our knowledge there is no published system that combines all three over PX4 logs. I say "to our
knowledge" on purpose; I cannot prove a negative.

The diagram shows my answer. Raw logs flow into a Parquet lake, into the canonical telemetry table, and into events.
The ontology sits beside them. Embeddings feed hybrid search. And the language model only ever sees curated read-only
views. It is one database, not five systems glued together.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 5. CORE CONTRIBUTION 1: SCHEMA DESIGN                                             (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: The central table is telemetry, and it is deliberately narrow: one row per timestamp, flight and canonical
field. The consequence is that a new signal adds rows, never columns, so the schema never changes when a new log
type arrives. It is a hypertable with seven-day chunks, and it currently holds 70.7 million rows.

Two tables make the mapping explicit: vendor_field and field_mapping. They record, for every vendor field, which
canonical concept it means, plus a scale, an offset and a note. The event table holds the text, a 1,536-dimension
embedding, and a generated tsvector column for full-text search. The screenshot at the bottom is the real DDL.

ANALOGY: The narrow format is like a ledger. You do not add a new column to the ledger for each new kind of expense;
you add a row with a category.

WHY IT MATTERS: as a plain rowstore, the index was 7.6 GB, larger than the 4.9 GB table. That observation motivated
the columnstore work on slide 10.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 6. CORE CONTRIBUTION 2: THE SEMANTIC LAYER                                        (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: The semantic layer is an ontology stored in ordinary relational tables: 63 concepts and 55 relations. The
chain is signal, subsystem, failure mode, event, and I walk it with recursive common table expressions. So a user
can say battery voltage instead of knowing which PX4 topic and field name holds it.

I also mapped a second autopilot, ArduPilot, by hand. I mapped attitude, GPS, position, velocity and battery. I
deliberately did not map vibration, motor outputs, EKF ratios, barometric altitude or flight mode, because they
mean different things across the two systems. A wrong mapping is worse than a gap, because it produces confident
wrong answers. The screenshots show a documented view and the mapping table with its reasons.

ANALOGY: The ontology is a translation dictionary between two dialects. A good dictionary says "no exact word" when
there is none, instead of guessing.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 7. METHOD 1: PIPELINES THAT SURVIVE FAILURE                                       (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: The data will grow from 300 logs to hundreds of thousands, so every batch stage is a job queue in Postgres. Each
item has a status, an attempt count, the last error and a lease time. Workers claim items with FOR UPDATE SKIP
LOCKED, which lets many workers run at once without fighting over the same row. Each item is idempotent and atomic:
its data and its status commit in the same transaction. One bad log never stops the run, and a crashed worker's
lease expires so another worker picks the item up. To scale, you enqueue more items; you do not change code.

An honest note: testing found that SKIP LOCKED once claimed 52 rows when I asked for 25, because the planner
re-ran the subquery. The fix is a materialized CTE. I found it by running concurrent workers, not by reading code.

ANALOGY: It is a deli counter with a ticket machine. Each customer takes one ticket, nobody serves the same ticket
twice, and if a server walks away mid-order the ticket goes back to the pile.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 8. METHOD 2: HYBRID SEARCH THAT ROUTES ITSELF                                     (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: Search combines three signals: vector similarity, full-text, and SQL filters, fused with reciprocal rank
fusion. The surprise is what happened with filters. With both indexes present and a filter keeping ten percent of
rows, Postgres chose HNSW with post-filtering. It returned only 4 rows out of the 10 requested, with recall of 0.37.
It was fast and wrong, because the cost model cannot know that the plan under-returns.

So the search routes itself. It asks the planner for the estimated number of filtered rows, using EXPLAIN on the
filter alone. If that is about 1,500 rows or fewer, it filters first and sorts exactly. Otherwise it uses HNSW with
an iterative scan. The flowchart on the right is exactly that decision.

ANALOGY: If only ten people in a stadium match your description, do not ask the crowd to point them out. Walk the
list of ten. If half the stadium matches, just ask the crowd.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 9. METHOD 3: GUARDED NATURAL-LANGUAGE SQL                                         (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: Letting a language model write SQL is risky, and "it only runs SELECT" is not a security boundary. So there are
four independent layers. One, a guard built on sqlglot: exactly one SELECT, allow-lists for tables and functions, and
the SQL that actually runs is regenerated from the parse tree, so what was checked is exactly what runs. Two, a
database role that can read only documented views. Three, a read-only transaction with an eight-second timeout.
Four, cost admission: I run EXPLAIN first and refuse anything with a planner estimate above 100,000.

I tested this with 46 hostile queries, and each was refused for the intended reason. A telemetry self-join, for
example, plans at 515 million and is rejected before it runs.

ANALOGY: Four locks on one door, each with a different key, so a thief who picks one still faces three.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 10. OPTIMIZATION: COLUMNSTORE PHYSICAL DESIGN                                     (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: This is experiment one. On 100 flights and 26.9 million rows I compared the rowstore with several columnstore
designs, taking the median of seven warm runs. Segmenting by flight and signal, ordered by time, shrinks the data
from 3.6 GB to 147 MB, 25 times, and the cross-flight query drops from about 1.5 seconds to 16 milliseconds.

The interesting part is what did not matter. Different compression settings change the size by under ten percent.
What does matter is chunk count and the segmentby choice, which change cross-flight speed by about two times. And
testing found a real bug: rows inserted into an already compressed chunk leave it partial, and my first compress
stage skipped those chunks. It now recompresses them.

ANALOGY: Segmenting by flight and signal is shelving books by author and title rather than by arrival date. To find
one author you open one shelf instead of scanning the building.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 11. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP                                                            (0:45)
======================================================================================================
SAY: All of this ran on four cores and 15 gigabytes of RAM with no GPU, in Docker. Versions are on the slide:
PostgreSQL 17.11, TimescaleDB 2.30.2, pgvector 0.8.7, pgvectorscale 0.9.1 and DuckDB 1.5.6. Timings use one
connection, a warm cache, and three measured passes or the median of five to seven runs. Vector recall is measured
against exact search with 200 held-out queries. For the language model I used a development set and a separate
held-out set, with bootstrap intervals and paired tests. Every mistake and its fix is in the decision log.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 12. RESULTS I: STORAGE AND ENGINE COMPARISON                                      (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: For all 70.7 million rows, the rowstore plus index takes 12.5 GB. The columnstore takes 385 MB, 32 times
smaller, at about 5.4 bytes per row. That matches sorted Parquet at 392 MB and beats DuckDB's own file at 700 MB.

Against DuckDB, the picture is mixed and I want to say that plainly. DuckDB's native table wins single-signal slices
and the full-scan aggregate. The columnstore wins pruned access, such as one flight's signals or a 90-day window,
and the whole-corpus per-flight summary, 1.0 seconds against 2.0 to 2.6. Postgres also gives concurrent writers,
transactions, roles and vectors in the same engine, which DuckDB does not. So DuckDB stayed a comparator. Loading
into the hypertable runs at 287 thousand rows per second.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 13. RESULTS II: VECTOR INDEXES AND FILTERED SEARCH                                (1:45)
======================================================================================================
SAY: On 100,000 vectors with 1,536 dimensions, exact search takes 362 milliseconds. HNSW with m equal to 32 reaches
0.95 recall in 2.4 milliseconds, over a hundred times faster. IVFFlat is slower to query but builds three to
seventeen times faster. DiskANN is twelve times smaller, 68 MB against 819, but did not reach 0.95 recall here.

The right table is the filter story. A post-filter with the default setting returns zero rows out of ten when the
filter keeps one tenth of one percent. Iterative scan fixes the row count. But an exact sort over a B-tree filter
takes 1 millisecond there and wins; at ten percent it takes 55 milliseconds and HNSW wins. The crossover is near a
thousand rows, which is why the router uses 1,500. I also re-ran the comparison on easier and harder synthetic
data, holding size fixed; the ranking did not change.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 14. RESULTS III: SEARCH QUALITY AND NL-TO-SQL                                     (1:30)
======================================================================================================
SAY: Two honest negative or nuanced results. First, I expected hybrid search to win. It did not. Vector only scores
0.710 nDCG at ten; plain rank fusion scores 0.600, significantly worse, with a paired difference of minus 0.109 and
an interval that excludes zero. A light full-text weight just ties vector only.

Second, for natural-language SQL on 27 held-out questions: semantic views score 0.89, raw tables with the same
documentation score 0.70, and raw tables alone score 0.26. The big effect is documentation, a gain of 0.44 that is
statistically clear. The views add another 0.19, but with so few questions that is not significant. I also report
that my first run overstated the gap because of bugs in my own comparison and guard; I fixed them, froze the
prompts, re-ran, and kept both runs.

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 15. THE INTERFACE                                                                 (0:45)
======================================================================================================
SAY: This is the application on top: a Next.js front end over a FastAPI back end. Left, the explorer. Middle, a
single flight, with a ribbon of flight modes and min-max envelopes so a spike can never be averaged away. Right,

======================================================================================================
SLIDE 16. CONCLUSIONS, LIMITS AND NEXT STEPS                                            (1:15)
======================================================================================================
SAY: To conclude. One PostgreSQL instance can hold the time series, the ontology, the vectors and a guarded
natural-language interface. Physical design cut storage 32 times, and filtering strategy mattered more than the
choice of vector index. Documentation drives language-model accuracy, and four independent layers keep it safe.
Hybrid fusion did not help, and I reported that as found.

The limits: 300 flights, one machine, one model, and questions written by me. The benchmark vectors are perturbed
copies of real ones. The snapshot tool is documented but untested end to end. Next is the large run on ten thousand
or more logs, ArduPilot at scale, and a concurrency benchmark. Thank you. I am happy to take questions.

======================================================================================================
Q&A PREPARATION
======================================================================================================

Q1. Why Postgres and not a dedicated time-series database or ClickHouse?
A. One engine gives transactions, roles, joins to the ontology and vectors together. I measured DuckDB as a
comparator; it wins some scans, loses on pruned access and the summary build. ClickHouse is future work; it would be
a new datastore and I would add it only deliberately.

Q2. Is 300 flights not too small for these conclusions?
A. Yes, that is a stated limit. Storage numbers scale predictably and are measured on 70.7 million rows. The vector
benchmarks use 50,000 and 100,000 vectors made by perturbing real ones. The full run is planned on a large machine,
and all volumes are configurable.

Q3. Why did hybrid search lose?
A. On 65 queries, vector similarity already handles paraphrases. Plain rank fusion lets weak keyword matches
displace good vector hits. With a small keyword weight it ties. I do not claim it never helps, only that I found no
evidence that it does on this data.

Q4. How can you call NL-to-SQL "safe"?
A. I call it defended, not proven. Four independent layers; 46 hostile queries each refused for the intended reason;
a fake model that follows prompt injection is still stopped. The residual risk is correctness, not safety:
confident wrong answers, which is why the answer shows its SQL and cost.

Q5. The views added 0.19 but you say it is not significant. Is it useful or not?
A. The direction is positive (six questions gained, one lost) but 27 questions cannot separate it from chance
(p = 0.125). I report it as suggestive. The clear effect is documentation.

Q6. What is the relative contrast number in the difficulty experiment?
A. It measures how much farther the tenth neighbour is than the nearest. Low means neighbours are hard to tell
apart. My synthetic sets were harder than the real corpus, so the index ranking is not an artefact of easy data.

Q7. What happens if a worker crashes mid-ingest?
A. The item's lease expires, another worker reclaims it, and because data and status commit together there is no
half-loaded flight. Completed items are never redone.

Q8. Why a narrow telemetry table instead of one column per signal?
A. New signals and new autopilots then need no schema change, and the ontology can map any vendor field. The cost
is more rows; the columnstore turns that into 5.4 bytes per row.

Q9. Why did the planner choose the wrong plan?
A. Its cost model estimates time, not result completeness. HNSW post-filtering looks cheap but returns 4 of 10
rows. The router avoids relying on that estimate for correctness.

Q10. What is untested?
A. The snapshot and restore script was deliberately left untested and is documented that way. The large-scale run
has not happened yet.
