====================================================================================================
SLIDE 1. TITLE   (0:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Good morning. My project is Semantic Log Analytics for UAV flight logs. The one-line version: I treated drone flight logs as a database problem instead of a plotting problem. Everything you will see runs on one PostgreSQL instance, and every design choice is backed by a measurement.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 2. MOTIVATION: FLIGHT LOGS ARE A DATABASE PROBLEM   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: A drone writes a log on every flight. In my data set, 300 real PX4 flights hold 70.7 million telemetry samples and 3,401 events such as warnings and failure flags. That is three kinds of data at once: a dense time series, free text, and vendor dialects, because PX4 and ArduPilot name the same quantity differently. Existing tools plot one log at a time, but the questions people ask span many flights and are asked in words, like which flights lost a motor. I wanted answers that are exact, explainable and safe to run on the data.
ANALOGY: A flight log is a black box recorder. Opening one recorder is easy. Asking which of three hundred recorders ever heard a motor fail needs a library catalogue, not a recorder player.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 3. RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND CONTRIBUTIONS   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Four research questions drive the talk, and each is answered by an experiment. One, how should high-rate telemetry be stored and at what cost. Two, which vector index and which filtering strategy retrieve events best. Three, can a model write safe and correct SQL, and what does a semantic layer add. Four, do the event detectors find faults of known size. The contributions are one schema for telemetry, ontology, events and embeddings; a measured columnstore design and engine comparison; a self-routing hybrid search with a filtered-search study; and a four-layer guarded natural-language SQL with a measured evaluation.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 4. BACKGROUND: TIMESCALEDB   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: TimescaleDB is an extension that makes Postgres good at time series. A hypertable looks like one table but is cut into time chunks. The columnstore stores old chunks column by column and compresses them, grouped by the segmentby columns and sorted by the orderby column. A continuous aggregate is a rollup the database keeps up to date; I use one for per-second minimum, maximum and mean. It fits telemetry because slowly changing sensor values compress to a few bytes, and a query for one signal of one flight reads one compressed segment instead of the whole table, while normal Postgres features such as joins, transactions and roles stay available.
ANALOGY: A hypertable is a filing cabinet with one drawer per week. The columnstore is vacuum-packing the old drawers.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 5. BACKGROUND: VECTOR SEARCH IN POSTGRES   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: pgvector adds similarity search. An embedding turns a piece of text into 1,536 numbers so similar meanings land close together. HNSW is a layered graph where you hop toward the query. IVFFlat sorts vectors into clusters and probes the nearest few. DiskANN from pgvectorscale is a compressed graph built for disk. We judge them by recall at k, the share of the true nearest neighbours returned. And there is a pitfall that becomes a headline result: the index finds neighbours first and the SQL filter is applied afterwards, so a selective filter can leave fewer rows than you asked for.
ANALOGY: HNSW is asking a friend of a friend for directions: each hop gets you closer and you never walk the whole city. The filter trap is asking for a vegetarian restaurant only after your friends have pointed you to five steakhouses.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 6. THE GAP AND THE ARCHITECTURE   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The gap: log viewers show one flight and know nothing about meaning; time-series stores keep the numbers but have no ontology or vendor mapping; natural-language SQL demos are rarely guarded or measured. To our knowledge no published system combines all three over PX4 logs, and I say to our knowledge on purpose. The diagram is my answer: raw logs become a Parquet lake, the canonical telemetry table and events; the ontology sits beside them; embeddings feed hybrid search; and the language model only ever sees curated read-only views. It is one database, not five systems glued together.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 7. THE DATA   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The corpus is 300 flights chosen, seeded and stratified, from 478,918 public Flight Review logs under a CC-BY licence: 8.75 GB of ULog files, parsed to a Parquet raw layer and the canonical table, giving 70,731,891 rows and 3,401 events. An ArduPilot MAVLink log goes through the same ontology to show the vendor mapping at work, and the gaps in that mapping are documented, not guessed.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 8. RELATIONAL SCHEMA   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: This is the schema. Flight has one row per log. Telemetry is a hypertable with one row per time, flight and field. Vendor field and field mapping connect a source field to a concept with a scale and an offset. Concept and concept relation hold the ontology. Event holds text, kind, time span, a 1,536-dimension embedding and a generated tsvector. Flight signature holds a 32-dimension signal-shape vector, note holds investigation notes, and llm call records every model call with tokens, cost and latency.
ANALOGY: The narrow telemetry table is a ledger: a new kind of expense adds a row, never a column.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 9. DDL: THE TELEMETRY HYPERTABLE   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Here is the real DDL. The narrow long format means a new signal adds rows, never columns, and a new autopilot needs no schema change, because the ontology maps vendor names to a vendor field id. Chunks are seven days. As a plain rowstore this table was a problem: the index was 7.6 GB, larger than the 4.9 GB table. That observation motivates the columnstore work.

SLIDE 10. INSPECTING THE HYPERTABLE: DIMENSIONS AND COLUMNSTORE SETTINGS   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: This is output from the running database, not a diagram. One time dimension on ts with seven-day chunks, 134 chunks in total, compression enabled, segmented by flight id and vendor field id, ordered by ts.

SLIDE 11. INSPECTING THE HYPERTABLE: CHUNKS   (0:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The chunk list: every chunk is compressed. The earliest ranges, 1970 and 2005, belong to logs whose device clock was implausible; the decision log, D6, describes how start times are guarded.

SLIDE 12. THE EVENT TABLE, AS THE DATABASE REPORTS IT   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The event table as Postgres describes it: the kind, the time span, severity, source, the description text, a generated tsvector for full text, and the vector column for the embedding.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 13. INDEXES ON THE SEARCH TABLES   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The indexes: an HNSW index on event embeddings of just 13 MB, a GIN index on the text vector, B-trees for the flight, kind and time filters, and a small HNSW index on the flight signatures. These are the building blocks the search experiments later compare.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 14. COLUMNSTORE DDL   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The columnstore is switched on with two settings. Segmentby of flight and field makes one compressed row group per signal of one flight, so reading one signal reads one group. Orderby ts gives the delta and run-length encodings long runs of similar values. Below is the compress stage. We found by testing that inserting into a compressed chunk leaves it partial, and my first stage skipped those chunks; it now selects uncompressed or partial chunks and does each in its own transaction.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 15. COLUMNSTORE DESIGN EXPERIMENT   (1:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Experiment one. On 100 flights and 26.9 million rows I compared the rowstore with several columnstore designs, taking the median of seven warm runs. Segmenting by flight and signal, ordered by time, shrinks the data from 3.6 GB to 147 MB, 25 times, and the cross-flight query Q2 drops from about 1.5 seconds to 16 milliseconds. What did not matter is also a result: the design choices change size by under ten percent. What does matter is chunk count and the segmentby choice, which change cross-flight speed about two times.
ANALOGY: Segmenting by flight and signal is shelving books by author and title instead of by arrival date: to find one author you open one shelf.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 16. STORAGE AT FULL SIZE: 32 TIMES SMALLER   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: At full size, the rowstore plus index takes 12,459 MB. The columnstore takes 385 MB, 32 times smaller, from 176 down to 5.4 bytes per row, with the same query results as the Parquet source. It matches sorted zstd Parquet at 392 MB and is 1.8 times smaller than DuckDB's own file, while keeping row-level ACID and live inserts.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 17. TIMESCALEDB AGAINST DUCKDB: QUERY LATENCY   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Experiment four compares TimescaleDB with DuckDB on the same 70.7 million rows, median of five warm runs. DuckDB's native table wins single-signal slices and the full-scan aggregate. The columnstore wins pruned access, such as every signal of one flight or a 90-day window, and the whole-corpus per-flight summary, 1.0 seconds against 2.0 to 2.6. I report both directions because the result is mixed.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 18. TIMESCALEDB AGAINST DUCKDB: LOADING AND VERDICT   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: On loading, Timescale COPY runs at 287 thousand rows per second. DuckDB bulk insert from Parquet is far faster at over six million, but row-by-row insert is 603 rows per second, which shows how engine-specific the load path is. The verdict: DuckDB is excellent for scans, but Postgres adds concurrent writers, transactions, roles and vectors in the same engine, so DuckDB stays a comparator.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 19. LOADING: ARROW TO CSV TO COPY   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: How the data gets in: columns are canonicalised in Arrow, with the scale and offset from the field mapping, and streamed as CSV into COPY, one transaction per flight.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 20. ONE QUERY, FROM THE DATABASE PAGE: HOW A SIGNAL IS READ   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: This is a real plan. A ColumnarScan reads only the compressed segments for one flight and one signal, buffer hits only, and a hash aggregate buckets them into one-second minimum, maximum and mean. A 901-bucket envelope takes 8.4 milliseconds.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 21. THE SEMANTIC LAYER   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The semantic layer is an ontology in ordinary relational tables: 63 concepts and 55 relations, made of 34 signals, 7 subsystems, 7 failure modes and 15 event kinds. The chain is signal, subsystem, failure mode, event, and I walk it with recursive common table expressions. Each vendor field maps to a signal with a scale, an offset and a note. So a query says battery voltage, not a vendor field name.
ANALOGY: The ontology is a translation dictionary between two dialects.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 22. DOCUMENTED SEMANTIC VIEWS   (1:00)
====================================================================================================
SAY: There are six views: flights, events, signals, signal summary, ontology and telemetry. They are the only objects the natural-language role can read, and their comments are the documentation the model's prompt is built from. That detail matters later, because documentation turns out to be the biggest effect in the accuracy experiment.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 23. A SEMANTIC VIEW DEFINITION   (0:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Here is one definition: events joined to their concepts so the model sees kind, severity and source names, never raw ids.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 24. ARDUPILOT MAPPING   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: For ArduPilot I mapped attitude, GPS, position, velocity and battery, 52 field mappings in total. I deliberately did not map vibration, motor outputs, EKF ratios, barometric altitude or flight mode, because they mean different things across the two systems. A wrong mapping is worse than a gap, since it produces confident wrong answers, while a gap produces an honest not available.
ANALOGY: A good dictionary says no exact word when there is none, instead of guessing.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 25. EVENTS: THREE SOURCES   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Events come from three sources: log transitions and warnings, the failure detector flags, and gaps-and-islands rules over telemetry. Gaps and islands is a window-function pattern that finds runs of consecutive abnormal rows. Rules fire only against a baseline in the same flight, near-duplicate messages collapse from 3,846 to 3,401, and flight duration uses a robust estimate because one garbage timestamp once made a flight ten to the thirteen seconds long.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 26. FAULT INJECTION: DO THE DETECTORS FIND FAULTS OF KNOWN SIZE?   (1:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Experiment: I held a constant fault value for a stated duration at a random time in a real flight, on 30 flights and 1,043 variants including controls, and asked whether the unchanged rule engine found the right event. Below each rule's minimum duration, two to three seconds, detection is near zero by design. From five seconds, 90 to 100 percent are found, within about a second of onset. The weakness is the battery rule: it raises false alarms on 23 percent of unmodified controls, against 3 to 7 percent for the others.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 27. FAULT INJECTION: THE TABLE BEHIND THE CHART   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The table behind the chart. The small detections at one to three seconds for the battery rule are matches elsewhere in the flight, with onset errors of 100 to 200 seconds, so they are not real detections.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 28. HYBRID SEARCH   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Search combines three signals: vector similarity, full text, and SQL filters. Event text is embedded with text-embedding-3-small into a vector column with an HNSW index; full text is a generated tsvector with a GIN index. Rankings are fused by reciprocal rank fusion, which adds one over k plus rank from each ranker, so rank, not raw score, decides. The default hybrid weights are 1.0 for vector and 0.1 for full text.
ANALOGY: When two judges score on different scales, you compare their rankings, not their scores.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 29. HYBRID SEARCH SQL   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The whole thing is one SQL statement: vector candidates, full-text candidates and filters are ranked separately and fused by RRF inside the database.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 30. SEARCH QUALITY: THE HYBRID DID NOT WIN   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: I expected hybrid search to win. It did not. On 65 concept queries, 39 of them hard paraphrases, vector only scores 0.710 nDCG at ten and a light full-text weight ties at 0.709. Plain rank fusion drops to 0.600, a significant loss, with a paired difference of minus 0.109 and an interval from minus 0.172 to minus 0.051. Ten exact-term lookups scored 1.000 for every method, and cosine distances sit in a narrow 0.40 to 0.60 band, so there is no honest relevance cutoff.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 31. VECTOR INDEXES AT 100,000 VECTORS   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Experiment two. On 100,000 vectors of 1,536 dimensions, exact search takes 362 milliseconds. The chart plots recall against median latency, up and to the left is better. HNSW with m equal to 32 reaches 0.95 recall in 2.4 milliseconds, over a hundred times faster than exact search. DiskANN never passes 0.74 recall here.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 32. VECTOR INDEXES: BUILD TIME, SIZE AND SPEED   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The table adds cost. HNSW builds in 56 to 149 seconds. IVFFlat builds in 8.5 to 17 seconds, three to seventeen times faster, but queries are slower. DiskANN is 68 MB against 819 MB, twelve times smaller, but loses recall on this data. So the choice depends on whether build time, size or latency matters most.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 33. DOES DATA DIFFICULTY CHANGE THE WINNER?   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: A fair worry is that my benchmark vectors are perturbed copies of real ones, so they might be too easy. I measured relative contrast, which says how much farther the tenth neighbour is than the nearest. The real corpus has 20.5, while my two synthetic sets have 6.0 and 2.0, so both are harder than real data. Holding size fixed at 50,000 and changing only the noise, HNSW with m 32 is still fastest at 0.99 recall in both and DiskANN still peaks below 0.9. The ranking is stable. An earlier comparison confounded size and noise, so this controlled run replaced it.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 34. FILTERED SEARCH: POST-FILTERING UNDER-RETURNS   (1:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Experiment three, the headline one. With the default setting the index scan returns 0.0 rows of the 10 requested when the filter keeps 0.1 percent of rows, and only 4.1 when it keeps 10 percent. Iterative scan restores the row count but not the base recall, and costs 50 milliseconds at 0.1 percent. An exact sort over a B-tree filter returns the right 10 rows in 1.0 millisecond there.
ANALOGY: If only ten people in a stadium match your description, do not ask the crowd to point them out. Walk the list of ten.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 35. FILTERED SEARCH: LATENCY AND THE CROSSOVER   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The latency chart shows the trade-off. Exact sort is fast when the filter is selective but costs 55 milliseconds at 10 percent and 329 at 50 percent, while iterative HNSW stays flat at a few milliseconds. The lines cross near 1,000 rows, which is what sets the router's threshold of about 1,500.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 36. THE PLANNER PICKS WRONG, SO THE SEARCH ROUTES ITSELF   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Here is the surprising part. With both indexes present and a filter keeping ten percent, Postgres itself chose HNSW post-filtering: 4.0 rows of 10, recall 0.37, in 1.6 milliseconds. It is fast and wrong, because a cost model estimates time, not completeness. So the search routes itself: it asks the planner for the estimated filtered row count with EXPLAIN on the filter alone. At about 1,500 rows or fewer it filters first and sorts exactly; otherwise it uses HNSW with an iterative scan.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 37. ROUTER CODE   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The decision itself: the estimate picks the exact path or the HNSW path, and the chosen path and the estimate are returned and shown to the user.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 38. ROUTER CODE: ESTIMATING THE FILTERED ROWS   (0:50)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The estimate comes from the planner via EXPLAIN in JSON, not from a count, so the filtered rows are not scanned to decide how to scan them.

SLIDE 41. NATURAL-LANGUAGE SQL: FOUR DEFENCES   (1:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Letting a model write SQL is risky, and only running SELECT is not a security boundary. So there are four independent layers. One, a guard built on sqlglot: exactly one SELECT, allow-lists for tables and functions, and the SQL that runs is regenerated from the parse tree so what was checked is what runs. Two, a database role that can read only the documented views. Three, a read-only transaction with an eight-second timeout. Four, cost admission: EXPLAIN first, and refuse anything the planner estimates above 100,000.
ANALOGY: Four locks on one door, each with a different key.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 42. GUARD CODE: STATEMENT CHECKS   (1:00)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The guard parses once and rejects anything that is not a single SELECT, any nested or recursive WITH, table-valued functions in FROM, and catalog-qualified names.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 43. GUARD CODE: ALLOW-LISTS AND THE REBUILT STATEMENT   (1:00)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Then tables and functions must be on the allow-lists, a sentinel row limit is injected, and the statement is regenerated from the tree.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 44. EXECUTION: ROLE, READ-ONLY TRANSACTION, COST ADMISSION   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Execution switches to the restricted role, opens a read-only transaction with timeouts, runs EXPLAIN first and refuses when the planner estimate exceeds the limit.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 45. THE ROLE BOUNDARY, TESTED IN THE RUNNING DATABASE   (0:50)
====================================================================================================

SLIDE 46. HOSTILE QUERIES   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: I tested 46 hostile queries covering stacked statements, file reads, COPY to a program, dblink, catalog access, sleeps and system settings. Each was refused for the intended reason, and 18 legitimate queries still pass. Realistic questions plan under 5,000, while a telemetry self-join plans at 515 million and is refused before it runs. SLIDE 47. DOES THE SEMANTIC LAYER HELP NL-TO-SQL?   (1:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Experiment five, on 27 held-out questions. Semantic views score 0.89 strict execution accuracy, raw tables with the same documentation 0.70, and raw tables alone 0.26. The big effect is documentation: 0.26 to 0.70, twelve questions gained and none lost, p of 0.0005. The views add 0.19 more, but with six gained and one lost, p of 0.125, that is not significant. Without documentation the model rarely fails loudly: 17 of 27 answers were confident and wrong, though it never fabricated an answer to an unanswerable question.

SLIDE 48. NL-TO-SQL EVALUATION: PROTOCOL AND HONESTY   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The protocol: a development set for prompt work and a separate held-out set scored once per condition, strict and lenient execution accuracy, and paired sign tests, using gpt-4o-mini behind an OpenAI-compatible client. The honesty note: my first run overstated the gap, 0.65 against 0.24, because my comparison treated numbers as strings and my guard rejected a safe operator. I fixed both, froze the prompts, re-ran, and kept both runs.

SLIDE 49. SIMILAR FLIGHTS BY BEHAVIOUR: SIGNAL-SHAPE SIGNATURES   (1:30)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Beyond text, I find flights that behaved alike. 145 statistical and spectral features over 30 signals are scaled and reduced by PCA to 32 dimensions, keeping 86 percent of the variance, and stored as a vector with an HNSW index. The sanity check is the share of a flight's five nearest neighbours on the same board: chance is 6.9 percent, scaled features 25.3 and the 32-dimension signature 25.0, so compression loses nothing measurable. It is a sanity check, not ground truth, since hardware influences sensor scale.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 50. CONCURRENT WORK IN THE DATABASE: SKIP LOCKED JOB TABLES   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: Batch stages run from job tables in Postgres. Each stage claims items with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, status, attempts and last error live in the row, and data and status commit together, so a crash returns only that item to the queue. Testing found that without a materialized CTE the planner claimed 52 rows for a limit of 25.

SLIDE 74. ENVIRONMENT AND METHOD   (1:00)

SAY: Everything ran on four cores and 15 GB of RAM with no GPU, in Docker: PostgreSQL 17.11, TimescaleDB 2.30.2, pgvector 0.8.7, pgvectorscale 0.9.1 and DuckDB 1.5.6. Timings use one connection, a warm cache, and three measured passes or the median of five to seven runs. Recall is against exact search with 200 held-out queries. For the language model I used a development set and a held-out set with bootstrap intervals and paired tests.

SLIDE 75. KEY RESULTS   (1:15)
====================================================================================================
SAY: The numbers to remember. Telemetry is 32 times smaller, 12.46 GB down to 385 MB. Cross-flight queries are up to 95 times faster. HNSW is over 100 times faster than exact search at 0.95 recall. The planner's wrong choice returned 4 of 10 rows. NL-to-SQL reaches 0.89 on held-out questions, plain rank fusion hurts, 46 hostile queries were refused, and 90 to 100 percent of injected faults are found once they last five seconds.

====================================================================================================
SLIDE 76. CONCLUSIONS AND LIMITS   (1:45)
====================================================================================================
SAY: To conclude. One PostgreSQL instance can hold the time series, the ontology, the vectors and a guarded natural-language interface. Physical design cut storage 32 times, and filtering strategy mattered more than the choice of vector index. Documentation drives language-model accuracy, and four independent layers keep it safe. Hybrid fusion did not help, and I reported that as found. The limits: 300 flights, one machine, one model, questions written by me, benchmark vectors that are perturbed copies of real ones, heuristic event thresholds, and a battery-low rule with 23 percent false alarms on controls. Next are 10,000 or more logs, ArduPilot at scale and a concurrency benchmark. Thank you; I am happy to take questions.

====================================================================================================
Q&A PREPARATION
====================================================================================================
Q1. Why Postgres and not a dedicated time-series database or ClickHouse?
A. One engine gives transactions, roles, joins to the ontology and vectors together. I measured DuckDB as a comparator: it wins some scans, loses on pruned access and the summary build. ClickHouse is future work; it would add a new datastore.

Q2. Is 300 flights not too small for these conclusions?
A. It is a stated limit. Storage numbers are measured on 70.7 million rows. The vector benchmarks use 50,000 and 100,000 vectors made by perturbing real ones, and I checked they are harder than the real corpus. A larger run is the next step.

Q3. Why did hybrid search lose?
A. On 65 queries, vector similarity already handles paraphrases. Plain rank fusion lets weak keyword matches displace good vector hits. With a small keyword weight it ties. I claim only that I found no evidence it helps on this data.

Q4. How can you call NL-to-SQL safe?
A. Defended, not proven: four independent layers, 46 hostile queries each refused for the intended reason, and a fake model that follows prompt injection is still stopped. The residual risk is correctness, which is why the answer shows its SQL and cost.

Q5. The views added 0.19 but you say it is not significant. Is it useful?
A. The direction is positive (six gained, one lost) but 27 questions cannot separate it from chance (p = 0.125). The clear effect is documentation.

Q6. What is relative contrast?
A. How much farther the tenth neighbour is than the nearest. Low means neighbours are hard to tell apart. My synthetic sets (6.0 and 2.0) are harder than the real corpus (20.5), so the index ranking is not an artefact of easy data.

Q7. Why a narrow telemetry table instead of one column per signal?
A. New signals and new autopilots then need no schema change, and the ontology can map any vendor field. The cost is more rows and a join; the columnstore turns that into 5.4 bytes per row.

Q8. Why did the planner choose the wrong plan?
A. Its cost model estimates time, not result completeness. HNSW post-filtering looks cheap but returns 4 of 10 rows. The router avoids relying on that estimate for correctness.

Q9. What is the weakest result?
A. The battery-low detector: it misses faults shorter than its minimum duration and raises false alarms on 23 percent of unmodified flights.

Q10. What has not been done yet?
A. The large-scale run on ten thousand or more logs. Everything in this talk was measured on the 300-flight corpus; the volumes are configurable.
