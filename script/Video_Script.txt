======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 1. THE PROBLEM AND THE IDEA (0:00 to 0:50)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: the deck title slide for 5 seconds, then the "Motivation" slide.
SAY: "Hello, I am Sumit Kumar. My project is Semantic Log Analytics for UAV flight logs. A drone writes a log on every flight. In my data set, 300 real PX4 flights hold seventy
point seven million telemetry samples and over three thousand text events such as warnings and failures. Existing tools show one log at a time, but the questions people ask
span many flights and are asked in words, like 'which flights lost a motor?' So I treated the whole collection as a database problem. Everything you will see runs on one PostgreSQL
instance, and every design choice is backed by a measurement."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 2. HOW IT IS BUILT (0:50 to 1:40)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: the deck's "The gap and the architecture" slide. Point at the diagram with the mouse in the order you speak.
SAY: "This is the architecture. Raw logs are parsed into a Parquet lake and into a telemetry table in TimescaleDB, which stores old data column by column, compressed. Rules and the
failure detector turn telemetry into events. Events are embedded and indexed with pgvector. An ontology, stored in ordinary relational tables, maps PX4 and ArduPilot field names
onto shared concepts like battery voltage. A FastAPI service and a Next.js application sit on top. The language model never touches the raw tables; it sees only curated, documented,
read-only views. Every batch stage runs from job tables in Postgres, claimed with SKIP LOCKED, so work is concurrent, resumable and survives failure."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 3. THE DATABASE: SCHEMA AND STORAGE (1:40 to 2:50)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: tab "Data explorer", Schema map. Hover the flight table so its links highlight. Click telemetry, then click the Structure tab. Then the Database page: the space bar and the plan.
SAY: "This is the data explorer, built into the app and read live from the database catalog. Flight is the center. Telemetry, events, signatures and signal windows all point to it. Telemetry is a
narrow table: one row per time, flight and signal, so a new signal or even a new autopilot never changes the schema. It is a TimescaleDB hypertable with seven-day chunks, compressed into the
columnstore, segmented by flight and signal. On the Database page you can see the result: seventy million rows take twelve point five gigabytes as a normal table, and three hundred eighty-five
megabytes in the columnstore, thirty-two times smaller. Below it is a real EXPLAIN ANALYZE of the query the charts use, reading only the compressed segments it needs. The Performance page shows the live cache hit ratio and the costliest statements."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 4. ONE FLIGHT: REPLAY AND CHARTS (2:50 to 3:40)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: the flight page (Flight 968d313d). Point at the behaviour strip. Click Replay at 16x and let it run about 15 seconds. Then pause, drag across the battery chart to zoom, then double-click to reset.
SAY: "Here is one flight. The ribbon shows the flight modes, and the red marks are critical events. I will replay it. The time cursor sweeps every chart and the ground track together, and events
surface as the replay passes them. Around six minutes forty the parachute fires. Above the replay is the behaviour strip: every ten seconds are compared with the same moments in all other flights, and the red windows are the ones unlike anything else; it lights up right after the failure. Clicking a red window lists the signal statistics that make it unusual. The charts are synchronized: I drag across one and all of them zoom. Server-side downsampling keeps minimum
and maximum envelopes, so a short spike can never be averaged away."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 5. INCIDENT REPORT WITH CITATIONS (3:40 to 4:35)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: click the "Incident report" chip. Show the headline and findings. Click one citation chip (for example E6) so the charts jump to that moment. Expand "Evidence". Type a question under "Ask about this flight" (for example: did the low battery cause the parachute to open?).
SAY: "This section writes an incident report. The important design point is that the numbers do not come from the model. The database gathers numbered evidence with SQL: the events, the
signals that are unusual compared with the whole corpus, and what similar flights did. The model only writes prose, and it must cite evidence items. Every citation is checked against the
evidence, and a statement with no valid citation is dropped. If I click a citation, the charts jump to that moment. Here the evidence shows a low battery, a failsafe, and the parachute
sequence."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 6. SIMILAR MOMENTS AND SEMANTIC SEARCH (4:35 to 5:30)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: "Similar moments": press Find similar moments, then Overlay on the first result. Then open Search and type "motor failure during takeoff".
SAY: "Each flight is also cut into overlapping ten-second windows, and each window is embedded by how every signal behaved. Here I pick a moment and search all flights for moments that behaved the
same way, with an HNSW index. Overlay draws the two matching windows on top of each other so you can see the match. I measured it: for events such as landing and takeoff, precision at ten is up to forty times higher than random. Semantic search works on the event text. It fuses
vector similarity, full text and filters. One finding surprised me: plain rank fusion was worse than vector search alone, so I report that as found. Filters were the real problem: with a
selective filter, pgvector can return no rows at all, so the search asks the planner how many rows the filter keeps and picks exact search or HNSW accordingly."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 7. ASK, THE WORKBENCH AND THE FOUR DEFENCES (5:30 to 6:45)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: Ask page: click the example "Which three boards had the most critical events?", open "How this was answered". Then the SQL workbench: run the prepared query, click Explain on it (show the plan tree), then type
SELECT pg_read_file('/etc/passwd') and run it to show the refusal. Then type DROP TABLE flight to show the second refusal.
SAY: "On the Ask page I write a question in English. The model writes SQL, but four independent layers stand between that SQL and the data. A parser-based guard allows one SELECT on allow-listed views and
rebuilds the statement from the parse tree. The query runs as a restricted role, in a read-only transaction with a timeout, and the planner's cost estimate is checked first. The page shows
the SQL, the cost and the guard actions. The SQL workbench lets me type my own queries under the same checks. Explain shows the planner's plan as a tree with its cost against the limit. A normal query runs. Reading a server file is refused by name. A DROP is refused.
I tested forty-six hostile queries, and each was refused for the intended reason."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 8. FLEET VIEWS: MAP, GALAXY, INCIDENTS (6:45 to 7:30)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: Map (hover over the dots; click a red one and show the card), then Fleet: click "Critical events" and "How unusual" under Flight galaxy, then Incidents: filter Kind to Failsafe activated and
click Open at on the first row. Then open Patterns (Failsafe activated, 1 min) for 5 seconds.
SAY: "These views use the whole corpus. The map places every flight that has a GPS fix, coloured by critical events. The flight galaxy places each flight by how it behaved, using a thirty-two dimension
behavioural signature projected to two dimensions, so I can colour it by board, firmware, or how unusual the flight is. The incidents feed lists every warning and failure across flights; I filter to failsafes
and open one at the exact moment. Patterns shows what usually happens before and after an event, computed live with a windowed self-join. The Rule lab lets me move a detector's threshold and see precision and recall against the autopilot's own warnings change immediately."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 9. THE EXPERIMENTS (7:30 to 9:00)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: Benchmarks page, scrolling slowly through: Vector indexes, Filtered search, Natural-language SQL, Columnstore, TimescaleDB against DuckDB. Pause on each chart for about 10 seconds.
SAY: "Every claim is an experiment, and they all live on the Benchmarks page, drawn from saved result files. For vector indexes at a hundred thousand vectors, HNSW reaches ninety-five percent recall in
two point four milliseconds, over a hundred times faster than exact search at three hundred sixty-two. For filtered search, the default post-filter returns zero of ten requested rows when the filter keeps one
tenth of a percent, which is why the router exists. For natural-language SQL, on twenty-seven held-out questions accuracy is point eight nine with the semantic views, point seven with documentation alone, and
point two six without it; the documentation is the big effect, and the extra benefit of the views is not statistically significant, which I say plainly. The columnstore design experiment and the comparison with
DuckDB show where each wins: DuckDB wins full scans, the columnstore wins pruned access, and Postgres keeps writers, roles and vectors in one engine."

======================================================================================================================
SEGMENT 10. WHAT WAS HARD, OUTCOMES AND LIMITS (9:00 to 10:00)
======================================================================================================================
ON SCREEN: the deck's "Key results" slide, then "Conclusions and limits".
SAY: "Three things were hard. First, correctness under concurrency: SKIP LOCKED once claimed fifty-two rows when I asked for twenty-five, until I used a materialized subquery. Second, honest evaluation: my first
language-model run overstated the benefit because of my own comparison bug, so I fixed it, froze the prompts and re-ran. Third, filtered vector search, which silently under-returns. The outcomes: storage thirty-two
times smaller, cross-flight queries up to ninety-five times faster, retrieval that routes itself, and a language interface that is safe by construction. The limits are real: three hundred flights, one machine, one
model, questions I wrote myself, and a battery-low detector with twenty-three percent false alarms. Next steps are a ten-thousand-flight run and a concurrency benchmark. Thank you."
